# Samples

Use these for the moves they show. The user's own passages, when they share them, come first and set the sound. Everything after them is secondary: take the move, never the rhythm, phrasing or old-fashioned word choice. Passages from other writers are public domain. For writers still under copyright, the move is described in plain words and nothing is quoted.

## The user's own work (outranks everything below)

The user's own writing is the only way to learn their sentence rhythm, since descriptions of other writers teach moves and can't reproduce the sound of a sentence. When the user wants a piece to sound like them, ask for three to five passages they sent that got the result they wanted, and match those first. Use them in the conversation only; never save them into the skill.

## Public-domain passages

**Lincoln, Second Inaugural Address (1865)**
> Both parties deprecated war; but one of them would make war rather than let the nation survive; and the other would accept war rather than let it perish. And the war came.

The move: state both sides honestly and at full length, then land the consequence in four plain words. The balance works here because the two sides are real. Traits: accountable, leaderly.

**Frederick Douglass, "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?" (1852)**
> This Fourth [of] July is yours, not mine. You may rejoice, I must mourn.

The move: speak to the audience in front of you, and use the occasion they came for as the evidence against them. Traits: provocative, accountable.

**Thoreau, Walden (1854)**
> Our life is frittered away by detail. An honest man has hardly need to count more than his ten fingers, or in extreme cases he may add his ten toes, and lump the rest.

The move: make an abstract claim, then prove it with a physical image and a joke. Traits: provocative, colorful.

**Seneca, Letter 1 to Lucilius (Gummere translation, 1917)**
> Nothing, Lucilius, is ours, except time. We were entrusted by nature with the ownership of this single thing, so fleeting and slippery that anyone who will can oust us from possession.

The move: open with the whole claim, addressed to one person, then give it a physical quality the reader can feel. Traits: plain, leaderly.

**Mark Twain, letter to George Bainton (1888)**
> The difference between the almost right word and the right word is really a large matter—'tis the difference between the lightning-bug and the lightning.

The move: explain an abstract point with two concrete things the reader has seen. Note the dash: it's from 1888 and it's the one place in this file where one belongs.

## Writers to study for moves (described, not quoted)

- **Warren Buffett's shareholder letters.** He reports bad years alongside good ones, names specific deals as mistakes and says what they cost. He explains finance with plain analogies and a dry joke. Traits: accountable, leaderly.
- **Paul Graham's essays.** He opens with a claim that sounds too simple or plain wrong, then earns it with short words and step-by-step reasoning, talking straight to "you." Traits: provocative, informative.
- **George Orwell's essays.** He takes a side, puts the evidence on the page, and prefers the short Saxon word to the Latin one. Traits: provocative, plain.
- **Joan Didion.** She opens on a scene or a hard fact and lets the reader find the meaning, with exact nouns and no setup. Traits: descriptive, colorful.
- **John McPhee.** He makes a technical subject vivid through one exact detail and a person doing real work. Traits: descriptive, informative.
- **Wendell Berry's essays.** Conviction grounded in a specific farm, county and way of working. Traits: accountable, provocative.
- **Doyle Dane Bernbach's 1960s Volkswagen ads ("Lemon", "Think small").** They lead with the product's flaw and turn it into the reason to buy, with a one- or two-word headline and body copy that talks like a person. Traits: provocative, colorful, short form.

Name a writer for one move at a time: "open on a scene, the way Didion does." Asking to "write like Didion" gets an impression of Didion.

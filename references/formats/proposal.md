# Proposals

## What the reader needs

The reader is deciding whether to spend money on you, usually while comparing you against other firms, so they read for five things: whether you understood their problem, what you'll do, what they'll get and when, what it costs, and why they should trust you with it. Everything in the proposal should answer one of those five questions.

## Modes

A proposal opens persuasive, frames the problem persuasively, runs its methodology in systematic mode and closes persuasive. A civic or public document opens persuasive, runs its structural sections as a cold diagnostic and closes persuasive.

The persuasive order is diagnosis first, then the earned right to propose, then specific proof. Open on the place or the problem, prove understanding through specifics the client hasn't told you (local history, site observations, the market), and present the capability as the natural answer. The systematic sections should give the client a named structure that holds, with parts they can repeat. A name like "Ten-Minute Wild" works where "the connection strategy" doesn't.

Format is strategy. A downtown plan might ship as a coffee-table book, a small pitch might win on seven pages with no padding, and a public park plan might need sixty pages of case studies. Choose the form for what the proposal has to survive.

## Shape

- Open with their situation in their own words and the result they're after. Your firm's history belongs near the end, if anywhere.
- State the recommendation plainly before the detail, so a reader who stops after the first page still knows what you're proposing.
- Write scope as deliverables with dates. "Guest journey maps for three segments, delivered in week 4" can be checked, while "a deep dive into your guests" can't.
- Put the price in one clear place. Default to one recommendation. Offer options only when the client asked for them or they differ in scope, and explain what each one leaves out.
- List assumptions and exclusions in plain sentences, because they prevent the arguments that sink projects later.
- Prove fit with named past work and a number or result from each project. Two strong cases beat a page of logos.
- End with the next step and a date.

Headers and tables are fine here, since proposals get scanned. Use a table for timeline and fees, and keep everything else in prose.

## Length

Match the length to the size of the decision. A small engagement fits on two pages, and a large one still needs a one-page summary up front.

## What to avoid

- Internal names for the writer's own methods. Describe what the method does and what the client gets from it. Names created for this client's project are part of the work and stay.
- Claims about process quality ("our proven approach", "a rigorous process"). Show the result the process produced instead.
- Hedged commitments ("we'll aim to", "we'll try to"). Say what you'll do, and name real risks separately with how you'll handle them.

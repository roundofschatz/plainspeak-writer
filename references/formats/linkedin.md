# LinkedIn posts

The post rules in this guide come from the research in linkedin-post-generator's `references/platform.md` ([github.com/roundofschatz/linkedin-post-generator](https://github.com/roundofschatz/linkedin-post-generator)), as of October 21, 2026. Where that research has a finding, it outranks this skill's style rules for posts, and where it has none, the skill's own rules stand. When LinkedIn changes, update linkedin-post-generator's `platform.md` first, then this guide.

## What the reader needs

The reader is scrolling. LinkedIn shows the first few lines and hides the rest behind "see more," so those lines decide whether anyone reads on. Open with the most specific thing the user gave you: a fact, a scene or the claim itself, and never a scene the user didn't describe. Never open with a greeting, a setup or "I've been thinking about".

In a post, the point lands in the first 140 characters, where the mobile app folds the post behind "see more." In 2026 data, story openers did best and contrarian claims came next, while question openers did worst, so open a post on a story or a claim, not a question. A story opens the post only when it stands for the post's whole point.

## Modes

A post opens in insightful mode when it offers a reframe, or persuasive mode when it makes a case, and closes conversationally. A headline is insightful and has to make sense the first time someone reads it. An About section opens insightful, frames capability persuasively and closes conversationally.

## Length and shape

- A post runs 1,300 to 1,900 characters, about 200 to 320 words. Posts of 1,300 to 2,500 characters did best in 2026 data, and posts under 400 did worst. When the user shares posts of their own or asks for a length, theirs wins.
- In a post, write paragraphs of one or two full sentences, and start a new one at each new point, usually 10 to 14 in a post. One dataset of about 400,000 posts found that posts with more than 14 paragraphs did 66% better than posts with fewer than 7. Every sentence is still a whole sentence that follows from the one before.
- In an About section, keep paragraphs short, one to three full sentences each, and don't write one sentence per line.
- No headers, bold text, emoji bullets or numbered lists unless the content is a list.
- End a post with one to three relevant hashtags on the last line, each naming the exact subject or a real community or event. One 2026 measure found that 1 to 3 gave about 9% more reach.

## Response posts

When the post answers an event, talk or someone else's post:

- Name the event and state what was said accurately, in the speaker's own words where possible. The post is only as strong as its fairness to the other side.
- Credit what the speaker got right before extending or disputing it.
- Argue with the idea on the record, never with a private exchange.
- Tag people only when you'd be glad for them to read how the post treats them.

## Endings

End on the position, a concrete ask, a next step, or a specific question about a named case or choice that someone in the field can answer in a sentence: "Did your county treat a heat pump swap under the 2024 code as a repair or as new work?" Skip the engagement bait: "Thoughts?", "Agree?", "Drop a comment below", "Who else has seen this?" and any request to comment, like or repost ("Comment YES if you agree", "Repost if this helped") all read as hunting for reach.

## Script check

Run the full check with the `linkedin` surface. On that surface V04 doesn't count one-sentence paragraphs, since a post's paragraphs run one or two sentences, so read an About section for one-line paragraphs yourself.

## What to verify before posting

Quotes, names, titles and numbers from a talk or transcript. Machine transcripts garble names and numbers often.
